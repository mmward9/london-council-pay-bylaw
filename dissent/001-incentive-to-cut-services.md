## The argument

A council with pay tied to the property tax levy has a personal financial incentive to hold the levy down, including by cutting services rather than by finding real savings. Deferring road maintenance, cutting library hours, freezing emergency-services hiring, and starving social programs would all show up as a flat or falling levy. Voters get a smaller tax bill and a longer-term service decay; council avoids a pay cut, or even claws one back. The bylaw turns council against its own constituents.

## The response

The bylaw does not pay council a bonus for cutting services. It has no upside at all. Section 4.5's accumulated decrement only ever subtracts from pay or leaves it unchanged; a levy cut erodes the decrement toward zero, but zero is the floor. There is no mechanism by which cutting services, or cutting the levy, produces pay above what the City's existing rules (census indexing, the periodic base reset) would already pay. A council chasing a raise through service cuts is chasing something this bylaw does not offer.

What the bylaw does offer is avoiding a further cut. That is a real incentive, and it is worth taking seriously rather than waving away. Two things blunt it.

First, the asymmetry runs the other way from what the objection assumes. A single year of aggressive service cuts is a visible, reportable political event with an immediate electoral cost, at the next election, for the specific councillors who voted for it. A pay decrement is diffuse, delayed, and shared across all fifteen seats. A council weighing "cut services now, avoid part of a pay decrement, and answer for it at the ballot box" against "hold services and accept the decrement" is not making a close call. The bylaw does not need to build in a brake against reckless cost-cutting. The next election already is one, and it is a faster and larger brake than anything a compensation formula could add.

Second, from the fifth fiscal year the bylaw applies onward, the target the decrement actually measures narrows. A levy increase up to the rate of inflation does not move the decrement at all, in either direction. Council can fund normal cost growth (wages, fuel, contracts, all rising with the rest of the economy) without touching pay, and does not need to cut a single service to avoid a decrement increase for keeping pace with inflation. The remaining incentive is specifically to avoid raising the levy *faster than inflation*, which is a materially narrower target than "avoid raising the levy at all," and correspondingly narrows any incentive to cut services beyond what inflation-adjusted budgeting already requires. For the first council term this bylaw applies, that narrower target is not yet in force; the rule is the original, uncompromising one (any levy increase costs pay, full stop), matched to the specific run of levy increases London has already had (8.7 percent, 7.3 percent, 3.4 percent) that prompted this bylaw in the first place.

The bylaw also does not require council to cut anything. A council that finds genuine operational efficiencies, restructures contracts, or pushes back against provincial cost downloads can hold the levy at or below the relevant threshold and pay nothing in decrement. The path of least resistance under this bylaw is competent management and, after the first term, ordinary cost discipline against inflation, not service decay.

If a future council is willing to wreck services to avoid a partial pay decrement it could otherwise absorb without much political cost, that council has problems no compensation structure fixes on its own. The voters, at the next election, are the actual constraint, and this bylaw's own transparency requirements (the Treasurer's mandatory annual publication of the levy change, the CPI figure once it applies, and the decrement) exist specifically so voters have the numbers to hold that election accountable.
