## The argument

A council with pay tied to property taxes has a personal financial incentive to hold taxes down, including by cutting services rather than by finding real savings. Deferring road maintenance, cutting library hours, freezing emergency-services hiring, and starving social programs would all let council hold the residential tax rate flat. Voters get a smaller tax bill and a longer-term service decay; council avoids a pay cut, or even claws one back. The bylaw turns council against its own constituents.

## The response

The bylaw does not pay council a bonus for cutting services. It has no upside at all. Section 4.5's accumulated decrement only ever subtracts from pay or leaves it unchanged; a rate cut erodes the decrement toward zero, but zero is the floor. There is no mechanism by which cutting services, or cutting the tax rate, produces pay above what the City's existing rules (census indexing, the periodic base reset) would already pay. A council chasing a raise through service cuts is chasing something this bylaw does not offer.

What the bylaw does offer is avoiding a further cut. That is a real incentive, and it is worth taking seriously rather than waving away. Three things blunt it.

First, the target the decrement measures is narrow. The bylaw does not ask council to hold the residential tax rate flat. A rate increase up to the annual change in the Ontario Consumer Price Index adds nothing to the decrement, in any year, from the first year the bylaw applies. Council can fund normal cost growth (wages, fuel, contracts, all rising with the rest of the economy) without touching pay, and does not need to cut a single service to avoid a decrement for keeping pace with inflation. The remaining incentive is specifically to avoid raising the municipal tax on an existing home *faster than inflation*, which is a materially narrower target than "avoid raising taxes at all."

Second, the meter is the rate, not the levy, so growth pays for growth. New homes joining the assessment roll lower the rate an existing home pays for a given amount of levy. A council that spends the revenue new construction brings in, and no more, holds the rate flat and owes nothing to the decrement. The bylaw does not ask council to starve a growing city; it asks council not to reach deeper into the pocket of the homeowner who was already here.

Third, the asymmetry runs the other way from what the objection assumes. A single year of aggressive service cuts is a visible, reportable political event with an immediate electoral cost, at the next election, for the specific councillors who voted for it. A pay decrement is diffuse, delayed, and shared across all fifteen seats. A council weighing "cut services now, avoid part of a pay decrement, and answer for it at the ballot box" against "hold services and accept the decrement" is not making a close call. The bylaw does not need to build in a brake against reckless cost-cutting. The next election already is one, and it is a faster and larger brake than anything a compensation formula could add.

The bylaw also does not require council to cut anything. A council that finds genuine operational efficiencies, restructures contracts, or pushes back against provincial cost downloads can hold the constant-assessment tax increase at or below CPI and pay nothing in decrement. The path of least resistance under this bylaw is competent management and ordinary cost discipline against inflation, not service decay.

If a future council is willing to wreck services to avoid a partial pay decrement it could otherwise absorb without much political cost, that council has problems no compensation structure fixes on its own. The voters, at the next election, are the actual constraint, and this bylaw's own transparency requirements (the Treasurer's mandatory annual publication of the residential rate in both years, the reassessment adjustment factor, the constant-assessment tax change, the CPI figure, and the decrement) exist specifically so voters have the numbers to hold that election accountable.
